# World prices, the rupee and duty priced Indian bullion

India's own government data show that three forces set domestic gold and silver prices over the past 80 years, in a fairly stable order of importance. First comes the world US-dollar price, then the rupee's long slide against the dollar, and last the wedge Indian policy drives between domestic and world prices. RBI's official Mumbai annual averages start only in 1970-71. Between then and 2025-26, **gold rose from Rs 184.96 to Rs 118,420.90 per 10 g (640 times, 12.47% a year)** and **silver from Rs 536.08 to Rs 162,672.25 per kg (303 times, 10.95% a year)**. Over the same span, wholesale prices rose about 32 times (all ratios DERIVED) ([RBI HSIE 2025-26, Table 35](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?id=23860); [RBI HSIE 2009-10, Table 41](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?id=12732)). RBI's own London and New York columns allow an exact split of that rise. The **world price multiplied gold by about 107 and silver by about 31**. **Rupee depreciation multiplied both by about 11.7–11.8**. A **shrinking domestic premium cut gold by about half and silver by about a sixth** (DERIVED).

Before 1990, Indian policy dominated. Import prohibition and Gold Control kept Mumbai gold **106.8% above London in 1970-71** (DERIVED from RBI columns), and the Finance Minister put the gap at **55–71% in 1990** ([Lok Sabha, 24 May 1990](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/810848/1/09_II_24051990_p293_p304_PII.pdf)). In the same era silver export bans pushed Mumbai silver **49% below New York in 1979-80** (DERIVED). Imports were liberalised in stages between 1992 and 1997. Since 2000-01 the gold premium has tracked the import duty almost one-for-one: **r = 0.95, slope 0.98** (DERIVED). Tariff moves are therefore the main domestic lever today, and geopolitics works through the world price and the rupee, as the Ministry of Finance told Parliament in December 2025 ([Lok Sabha USQ 2479](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/3018040/1/AU2479_CMXSBj.pdf)).

For 1946–1969 no official yearly gold series could be read; those years are GAP apart from dated points and two ministerial figures. For silver, RBI averages survive only for 1950-51 and 1955-56 to 1958-59, and they were read on a non-government mirror server. For 2026-27 to date (April–August), RBI monthly averages put **gold at Rs 149,723.66/10 g and silver at Rs 239,733.63/kg, up 55.3% and 129.7% on the same months of 2025** (DERIVED). RBI Sovereign Gold Bond prices show 999 gold **7.85% lower on 13 November than on 23 April 2026**.

## 1. Executive summary: policy ruled before 1990, the world price after

**1946–1962: an insulated, pegged market.** The rupee was pegged and barely moved. RBI's exchange-rate FAQ puts the fiscal-year average at Rs 3.3175/USD in 1947-48, Rs 4.0744 in 1949-50 (the devaluation year) and Rs 4.7750 in 1950-51, and it stayed near Rs 4.76–4.79 until 1965-66 ([RBI FAQ](https://rbi.org.in/commonman/English/scripts/FAQs.aspx?Id=1877)). The few official prices that survive describe a flat market:

- Bombay gold peaked at **Rs 121 per fine tola (Rs 103.74/10 g) in August 1948**, and again at Rs 118.62 per tola (Rs 101.70/10 g) after the Korean boom in April 1951 ([RBI Report on Currency and Finance 1958-59, read via a non-government mirror host](https://www.newbank.sit.internal.pwc.in/documents/87730/30842423/23CF10062020_1958ea7620767f84444b93fd9820d86d0838.pdf)).
- The Finance Minister told Parliament in 1964 that gold went from **Rs 97.70 to Rs 119.59 per 10 g between 1950 and 1962**, "an increase of hardly 22 per cent" ([Lok Sabha, 21 Dec 1964](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/879841/1/03_X_21-12-1964_p88_p120_PII.pdf)).
- Silver averaged Rs 159.37/kg in 1950-51 and **Rs 144.65–162.95/kg in 1955-56 to 1958-59** (same RBI report, same mirror-host caveat).

No source fetched ties Partition, the 1949 devaluation or the Korean War to a measured Indian price effect. The one exception is the RBI report's own phrase, "post-Korean peak".

**1962–1990: war, Gold Control and smuggling economics.** The 1962 China war brought three measures: gold bonds, a ban on gold forward trading (13 Nov 1962) and the Gold Control Rules from 10 January 1963. The Finance Minister later said **prices fell about 30% within three months** ([Lok Sabha, 22 Jul 1968](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/872171/1/04_V_22-07-1968_p168_p179_PII.pdf)). Control suppressed legal supply but not demand, so it created a smuggling premium. RBI's first annual figures, for 1970-71, show Mumbai gold at Rs 184.96 against a London-equivalent Rs 89.44: **a premium of 106.8%** (DERIVED from [Table 41](https://www.rbi.org.in/scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?id=12732)). That premium made the domestic price sticky. Before 1990, the year-to-year premium change moved almost exactly opposite to the world price (**r = −0.84 for gold, −0.94 for silver**, DERIVED). The premium shrank when world prices spiked (1973-74, 1979-81) and widened when they fell (1981-82). Even so, the end of the dollar–gold link in 1971 leaked through: **Mumbai gold rose 6.3 times in the 1970s and posted 21 straight yearly rises, 1971-72 to 1991-92** (DERIVED). Inflation also mattered in this era: gold's yearly change correlates **0.78 with WPI inflation in 1971-72 to 1989-90, but −0.05 after 1990** (DERIVED).

Silver behaved in the opposite way. India has "no primary source of silver" ([Lok Sabha, 31 Mar 1978](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/920814/1/06_IV_31-03-1978_p27_p27_s553.pdf)), so the price depended on trade rules. An export window ran from February 1974 until a ban "to conserve the stock of silver" on 20 February 1979 ([Lok Sabha, 23 Mar 1979](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/920144/1/06_VII_23-03-1979_p27_p28_s467.pdf)). From 1973-74 to 1983-84 Mumbai silver sat at or below New York, 49% below in 1979-80. Then, with the ban still in force, it rose far above New York, +162% by 1990-91 (DERIVED).

**1990–2012: liberalisation and convergence.** The reforms came in a short burst:

- Gold Control was repealed in 1990 ([Lok Sabha, 24 May 1990](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/810848/1/09_II_24051990_p293_p304_PII.pdf)).
- The Gulf oil shock tipped India into the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis, and RBI shipped **46.91 t** of gold to the Bank of England ([Lok Sabha, 18 Jul 1991](https://eparlib.sansad.in/bitstream/123456789/7941/1/10_I_18071991_p152_p153_t141.pdf)).
- The rupee's FY average went from Rs 17.94 (1990-91) to Rs 24.47 (1991-92) and Rs 30.65 (1992-93) ([RBI HBS 2017, Table 148](https://rbi.org.in/scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?id=17923)).
- Returning Indians could import 5 kg at Rs 450/10 g from February 1992 ([Budget 1992-93](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/doc/bspeech/bs199293.pdf)).
- Open General Licence (OGL) imports through 11 nominated agencies followed in October 1997 ([Economic Survey 1997-98](https://indiabudget.gov.in/budget_archive/es97-98/chap65.pdf)).

The gold premium fell from 59.5% (1990-91) to 15.1% (1997-98), 8.0% (1998-99) and **about 1% in 2006-07 to 2011-12** (DERIVED). From there the world price did the work: Mumbai gold rose **5.75 times between 2000-01 and 2011-12, silver 7.28 times** (DERIVED).

**2012–2026: duty as a current-account tool, then a safe-haven super-cycle.** In 2012–13 duty rose in steps from 2% to 10% to curb imports that had pushed the current-account deficit to **4.81% of GDP in 2012-13** ([RBI HSIE Table 241](https://rbi.org.in/scriptS/PublicationsView.aspx?id=24066); [RBI Annual Report 2012-13](https://rbi.org.in/Scripts/AnnualReportPublications.aspx?Id=1079)). The gold premium rose to 13.4% in 2013-14 (DERIVED). The government attributes the 2011, 2020, 2022 and 2025 rallies to global safe-haven demand. 2025-26 brought **record annual gains: gold +56.1%, silver +82.5%**, against world gains of +52.4% (London) and +74.7% (New York) in dollars (DERIVED). In 2026 the West Asia war worked mainly through the rupee, which hit record lows, and through policy: duty went from 6% to 15% on 13 May, and silver imports became "Restricted" on 16 May.

The international prices are used only for those two calculations, never as Indian prices.

### 2.2 Caveats the reader must keep in view

- **Series break.** RBI flags a source and comparability break between 1999-2000 and 2000-01. Before the break the source is the Bombay Bullion Association and the Press Trust of India; after it, the financial press, IBJA, LBMA and Thomson Reuters/IMF-IFS. RBI's note reads: "Data provided in this Table for the period 1983-84 to 1999-2000 and 2000-01 to 2025-26 may not be strictly comparable" ([Table 35](https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/PublicationsView.aspx?id=23860)).
- **Purity.** No RBI table states purity. The Bulletin labels the series "Standard Gold". SGB prices are 999, and Income Tax rates are 995.
- **Fiscal years.** All RBI figures are April–March averages. RBI publishes no calendar-year averages. The calendar-2025 figures below (gold Rs 101,560.92/10 g; silver Rs 120,294.56/kg) are DERIVED as unweighted means of RBI monthly averages. That is the same method that exactly reproduces RBI's FY2025-26 gold average of 118,420.90.
- **Units before metrication.** RBI's 1958-59 tables quote gold "per fine tola" and silver "per 100 tolas". These are converted at 1 tola = 11.6638 g: Rs/10 g = Rs/tola × 0.857354, and Rs/kg = Rs per 100 tolas × 0.857354. The Income Tax Department gives 1 tola = 11.664 g.
- **Edition disagreements and internal inconsistencies.**
  - For 1998-99 the 2009-10 edition prints whole-rupee values (4268.00 / 7855.00); later editions print 4268.17 / 7855.33, which are used here.
  - In four years (1996-97, 1998-99, 2000-01, 2019-20) the printed gold spread differs from Mumbai minus London by Rs 0.16–1.66, so premiums here are computed from the prices.
  - The Aug-2025 edition printed a 2024-25 silver spread of 11,700.17, which was an error; the 2025-26 edition prints 6,445.43.
  - A 2016-dated monthly table shows a 2016-17 "average" (gold 29,786.11) that is probably part-year; the full-year value 29,665.28 is used.
- **Why gaps remain.** Every rbidocs.rbi.org.in PDF and XLSX returned a CAPTCHA, website.rbi.org.in links redirected to the RBI home page, and the RBI HSIE archive links for 2001–2008 are placeholders. The only readable copy of the 1958-59 *Report on Currency and Finance* sits on a PwC staging mirror of RBI's site. It is an RBI document but **not a government host**, so every figure from it carries †. Its gold annual rows (Statement 50) have no numbers in the OCR.
- **Exchange rate.** RBI-published FY averages exist for 1946-47 to 1963-64 and 1974-75 onward (official rates up to 1991-92; from 1992-93, after the move to a market-determined rupee, they are RBI-published market-based rates, labelled "official" in the tables below only to distinguish them from DERIVED values). Values for 1965-66 to 1970-71 are DERIVED by inverting RBI's "USD per Rs 100" figures. 1964-65 and 1971-72 to 1973-74 are GAP. For those three years the master table also shows the rate implied by RBI's own gold columns (DERIVED). Across 1970-71 to 2025-26 that implied rate is within 0.17% of the official one on average (maximum 1.1%).
- **Inflation.**
  - WPI annual rates are official throughout (1953-54 onward). For 2012-13 to 2014-15 the official 2011-12-base rates are used, not the earlier 2004-05-base values.
  - CPI-IW rates are official from 1993-94 and DERIVED from index levels for 1972-73 to 1992-93. For 2023-24, the HSIE prints 5.1 and the RBI Annual Report 5.2.

### 2.4 Method

Every computed figure is labelled **DERIVED** (or "D") and was produced in Python (pandas 3.0.5, numpy 2.5.3, scipy 1.18.1) from the values recorded in the notes:

- **Year-on-year (YoY) change:** FY average ÷ previous FY average − 1.
- **Gold/silver ratio:** (gold Rs/10 g × 100) ÷ silver Rs/kg. This is the number of grams of silver that buy one gram of gold.
- **Premium:** Mumbai price ÷ RBI's London (gold) or New York (silver) price in rupees − 1.
- **Implied exchange rate:** London Rs/10 g ÷ (London $/oz × 10/31.1034768).
- **Decomposition** uses the exact identity ln(Mumbai) = ln(world USD price) + ln(implied INR/USD) + ln(Mumbai ÷ world-in-rupees). The residual is below 10⁻¹² in every year.
- **Real growth** deflates by WPI, chaining the official annual rates.
- **Statistics:** correlations are Pearson and Spearman, and tests between groups use Welch's t-test.

The CSV and `chart_data.json` hold the identical numbers.

### 4.2 How the transmission changed, era by era

**Before 1971, global events barely registered.** The $35 peg held world gold still, and India's import ban walled the domestic market off. The shocks that moved Indian prices were India's own responses to war: the 1962 controls (down about 30%) and the 1966 devaluation (ministers said about 57% up). The world events of the 1960s (the 1960 London run, the 1961 Gold Pool, its 1968 collapse) left no documented Indian price trace. Their legacy shows up instead in the 106.8% premium RBI's data imply for 1970-71.

**In 1971–1990 the world price leaked in, filtered by a sticky premium.** Once the dollar–gold link broke, rising world prices fed Indian prices through smuggling economics; the government said smuggling *in* declined from 1971 because prices abroad had risen. The government also conceded that the late-1978 world spike lifted domestic prices enough to force RBI to stop its anti-smuggling auctions. Domestic prices were nonetheless far smoother than world prices. In 1979-80 London gold rose 94.6% in dollars but Mumbai only 46.5%, and in 1981-82 London fell 28.0% while Mumbai rose 12.9%. The premium was the shock absorber: before 1990 its yearly change correlates −0.84 with the world price for gold and −0.94 for silver (DERIVED). That is why Indian gold never fell in a year between 1971-72 and 1991-92.

**From 1990 onward the world price passes through almost fully, and the rupee magnifies crises.** After liberalisation the premium stopped absorbing world moves (correlation −0.02 for gold), and the yearly INR change tracks the world USD change closely. The R² is 0.72 for gold and 0.81 for silver in 1990-91 to 2025-26 (DERIVED). The government's own explanations shift accordingly:

- crisis asset in 1991;
- "safe haven" demand in 2011-12, 2020-21 and 2021-22;
- "heightened geopolitical tensions" plus central-bank buying in 2025.

Crises that hit India's balance of payments added a rupee leg on top of the world price. In 2008-09 rupee weakness contributed about as much as the world price (13.4 vs 12.4 log-points). In 2013-14 it offset most of a 22-point world fall. In 1991-92 it was the whole story.

**2026 broke the usual pattern.** Normally a war lifts world gold. The 2026 West Asia war instead strengthened the dollar, and RBI says gold "receded in March" while the rupee hit record lows. In rupee terms, Mumbai gold slipped only from 154,038.52 (Feb) to 149,927.30 (Apr), because the rupee's slide cushioned the fall. Domestic policy then took over: the 13 May duty increase came while the RBI monthly average rose 3.2% from April to May (DERIVED). After that, the Fed's hawkish turn (non-government reporting) and the fall in RBI SGB prices to Rs 14,024/g by 13 November mark a softer second half. Silver followed the same path with roughly double the amplitude. It peaked in January 2026 and was 19.6% lower by August (DERIVED).

### 5.4 What the policy record shows

**Prohibition made gold dear and silver cheap, and supply schemes only dented the gap.** Every pre-1990 measure aimed at the same thing: keep gold out and keep silver in, to save foreign exchange. Gold therefore traded far above world prices:

- 106.8% above London in 1970-71 (D);
- an average of about 48% in both 1970-71 to 1979-80 and 1980-81 to 1991-92 (D);
- 55–71% in 1990, per the Finance Minister.

The supply-side remedies worked only briefly. The 1978 auctions cut the gap from Rs 160 to Rs 77 per 10 g in three months, then were overwhelmed when world prices rose. Bonds and amnesties (1962, 1965, 1993) drew little gold: about Rs 7 crore and Rs 60 lakh respectively, in the early tallies members cited. For silver the export ban produced the reverse wedge until the early 1980s.

**Liberalisation replaced the smuggling premium with a duty wedge.** The 1992 passenger scheme was priced explicitly to undercut smugglers: Rs 450/10 g, roughly 15% ad valorem, against a premium of 55–71%. OGL in 1997 finished the job. Section 7.5 shows that from 2000-01 the premium equals the time-weighted duty almost exactly (mean gap +0.44 percentage points for gold). Every duty change since then has been a domestic price change of roughly the same size:

- 2012–13: +2 to +10 points, in steps;
- 2019: +2.5;
- 2021: −2.5;
- 2022: +5;
- 2024: −9;
- 2026: +9.

NITI Aayog recorded the cost of this: hikes "reduced official imports but increased unofficial ones" ([NITI Aayog 2018](https://niti.gov.in/sites/default/files/2019-06/Report_GoldMarket.pdf)). Seizures rose with duty, from 2,154.58 kg (2020) to 3,502.16 kg (2022) ([LS USQ 1680](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/1469590/1/AU1680.pdf)).

**The public resisted coercion and adapted to price.** The record from 1963–64 shows mass protest: about 2 lakh memoranda and a petition said to carry a crore signatures. It also shows an admitted "aversion to 14 carat ornaments". An MP observed that "the love of gold has not decreased" ([LS 21 Dec 1964](https://eparlib.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/879841/1/03_X_21-12-1964_p88_p120_PII.pdf)). Six decades later the response to record prices is to change how people buy rather than whether they buy. Non-government sources report lighter-carat jewellery (9K–18K), 1–5 g coins, old-gold exchange and silver as a substitute. They also report pledging rather than selling: bank gold loans roughly doubled from May 2024 to May 2025 (Rs 1,16,777 crore to Rs 2,51,369 crore, per a Lok Sabha reply reported by the press). Short panics follow sudden government action: before the 8 February 1963 deadline, on demonetisation night in 2016, and in the October 2025 silver rush. None of them changed annual RBI averages in a way the data can isolate.

**Silver was a policy afterthought until 2025.** It shared gold's duty steps from 2013, with one exception: lower duty until 2 February 2023, when the Budget aligned it "with that on gold and platinum" (L). The first silver-specific rules came in 2025: RBI collateral caps (10 kg ornaments, 500 g coins), silver HUID hallmarking from 1 September 2025, and restrictions on plain silver jewellery imports (L). Silver imports became "Restricted" on 16 May 2026.

## 8. Silver-specific drivers and the gold–silver ratio

**Silver's Indian history is a trade-policy story.** With no primary mines, India first locked silver in: export bans to 1974 and again from 20 February 1979, policed against outward smuggling. It then let silver in, from the 1993 passenger scheme (G-sec), OGL in 1997 and a structural import surplus after that. RBI's columns show the result. Mumbai silver was **at or below New York from 1973-74 to 1983-84**, by up to 49.2% in the Hunt spike year 1979-80. Then, with the ban still in force and world silver falling, it **rose far above New York: +27.9% (1984-85), +91.3% (1986-87) and +162.1% (1990-91)** (D). No fetched government source explains the flip. A plausible inference is that a closed market, with neither exports nor commercial imports, priced off domestic demand while world silver fell. The premium collapsed to 33.9% in 1993-94 and 10.0% in 1998-99 as imports opened. Since 2000 silver's premium has tracked duty, about 1.8 pp above it.

**Silver is gold with a higher beta, plus episodes of its own.** After 1990 silver's yearly SD is 21.8% against 14.2% for gold, and it fell in 12 of 36 years against 8 for gold (D). It beat gold in 22 of 55 years. Its biggest outperformance came in global silver-specific rallies: 2006-07 (+61.1% vs +33.9%), 2010-11 and 2011-12 (+47.3% and +53.7%), 2020-21 and 2025-26 (+82.5% vs +56.1%). It underperformed in 1977-78 (STC export liquidation), after the Hunt collapse (1980-82), in 1993-94 (import opening), and in the long 2013-14 to 2019-20 bear market.

2025–26 had silver-only features:

- a physical shortage before Dhanteras that took RBI's monthly silver premium to 11.0% in October 2025 (D) while the gold premium stayed at 5.8%;
- a peak RBI monthly average of Rs 285,592.95/kg in January 2026, 217% above January 2025 (D);
- a fall of 19.6% from January to August 2026 (D).

On the policy side came the first silver-specific import controls (jewellery in September 2025, bars in May 2026; L and C respectively). The Economic Survey 2025-26 framed both metals as safe havens and said nothing on industrial demand (G-sec, [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/business/economic-survey-2026-gold-silver-prices-to-remain-elevated-amid-global-uncertainties-3878659)).

From 1970-71 to 1984-85 the Indian ratio ran **7 to 27 points above** the world ratio. Indian gold was dear (smuggling premium) and Indian silver cheap (export controls). From 1986-87 to 1992-93 it ran **16 to 32 points below**, because Indian silver became dear in a closed market. After the 1993 import opening it converged, and since 2019-20 the two coincide almost exactly (mean gap −0.1). The level correlation between them rises from 0.43 (to 1991-92) to 0.90 (1992-93 onward) (D).

Only two pre-1970 spot ratios can be computed, both from the RCF 1958-59 (mirror host†): **56.9 on 31 March 1958** and **58.4 on 10 July 1958**, the date of both metals' lows that year (D). The ratio's extremes in the RBI series are:

- **34.5 in 1970-71**, the series low;
- **44.9 in 2011-12**, the silver spike;
- **87.1 in 2019-20**, the series high, after six years of silver weakness;
- **72.8 in 2025-26**.

The monthly series shows how far the squeeze went: the ratio fell from **98.5 in May 2025 to 51.1 in January 2026**, then recovered to 66.7 by August 2026 (D). For context only, non-government Delhi spot quotes imply about 45 at the 29 January 2026 peak. The ratio's percentage change correlates −0.60 with silver's yearly change: the ratio is mainly a silver story.

## Conclusion

This record changes the usual Indian story about gold. Tradition, weddings and festivals explain why Indians hold some 20,000+ tonnes. They do not explain the yearly price. On RBI's own numbers, **the world price explains roughly 70% of yearly moves in Indian gold and 80% in silver since 1990**. Domestic policy's influence is narrow but precise: the import duty now sets the domestic premium almost point for point. That makes the duty a real price lever, but also a smuggling lever, as the seizure data show. The rupee seldom drives a single year, yet over decades it has multiplied rupee bullion prices nearly twelve-fold, and in balance-of-payments crises it becomes the main driver. Inflation-hedging worked as an Indian price driver only when the market was closed; since 1990, Indian gold has been a dollar asset converted at the rupee rate.

Going forward, the duty is the variable to watch. The 13 May 2026 rise to 15% reverses the July 2024 cut. On the post-2000 relationship it should lift the domestic premium by roughly the nine-point duty increase. RBI international columns that would confirm this are not yet published, and non-government reports instead describe a record discount and renewed smuggling. That tension, between a policy-set premium and a market that routes around it, is the same one Gold Control created in 1963. For silver, the new import licensing adds a quantity control for the first time since the 1979 export ban, so the Indian silver ratio may again drift away from the world ratio. The data needed to check both are RBI's next Handbook and Bulletin releases, together with the RBI archive scans that would finally fill 1946–1969.
